## Family

In the Puranic stories, Shani Dev is the son of **Surya**, the Sun god, and **Chhaya**, whose name means "shadow".

:::belief The story of Chhaya
According to tradition, Surya's first wife Sanjna (also called Saranyu) found his brilliance too intense to bear. Before leaving to perform austerities, she created her own shadow, Chhaya, to take her place. Shani was born to Chhaya. In many retellings, Surya doubted the dark-complexioned child, and the tension between father and son became part of Shani's story — one reason he is sometimes associated with the pain of rejection and with the dignity of those who are overlooked.
:::

His siblings, through Sanjna, include **Yama**, the lord of death and justice, and **Yamuna**, the river goddess. The connection with Yama — another figure of cosmic justice — fits Shani's own reputation as a judge of actions.

## Iconography

Shani Dev is depicted in several ways, but some features recur:

| Feature | Common depiction |
| --- | --- |
| Complexion | Dark — black or deep blue |
| Clothing | Black or dark blue garments |
| Vehicle (*vahana*) | A crow; sometimes a vulture or a chariot |
| Weapons | Bow and arrows, sword, trident or mace |
| Posture | Often seated on his vehicle, sometimes with one hand raised in blessing |
| Planet | Saturn |
| Day | Saturday (*Shanivar*) |

In some images he is shown as lame, walking slowly — an image linked to his name "the slow one" and to a story in which his leg was injured.

At Shingnapur there is no carved image at all: Shani Dev is worshipped as a dark stone, regarded as self-manifested. See the [temple overview](/temple/).

## Symbols and associations

Across regions, devotees commonly associate Shani Dev with:

- **Black and dark blue** colours
- **Sesame** (black sesame seeds and sesame oil)
- **Iron**
- **Mustard or sesame oil lamps**
- **Crows** — feeding crows on Saturdays is a folk practice in some places
- **The *shami* tree**, in some traditions

These associations shape the offerings described in our [pooja guide](/temple/pooja/).

## Well-known stories

:::belief Stories from tradition
The stories below are told in Puranic literature, regional texts and popular devotional retellings. Details differ widely between versions.
:::

### Shani's gaze

A widespread belief holds that Shani's gaze (*drishti*) is powerful and can bring misfortune. One well-known story tells that when Shani looked upon the infant Ganesha, the child's head was severed — one of several stories explaining Ganesha's elephant head. The theme reflects how intensely Shani's influence is imagined.

### Hanuman and Shani

In a popular story, Hanuman freed Shani from captivity (in many versions, Ravana's). In gratitude, Shani promised not to trouble Hanuman's devotees. This is why many people pray to Hanuman on Saturdays, and why Hanuman shrines are often found near Shani shrines.

### King Vikramaditya

Folk narratives, particularly in western India, tell of King Vikramaditya, who doubted Shani's power and endured years of misfortune before recognising his error and being restored. The story is often recited as a lesson in humility.

### King Dasharatha

The *Shani Stotra* attributed to King Dasharatha, father of Rama, is associated with a story in which Dasharatha confronts Shani to protect his kingdom from famine, and Shani, pleased by his courage and devotion, grants his request. See [Shani stotra](/shani-dev/stotra/).

## A balanced view

Popular culture often presents Shani Dev only as a figure to be feared. Devotees tend to see him differently: as a guardian of justice who rewards honesty, hard work and compassion, and whose "difficult" periods are seen as opportunities for growth.
