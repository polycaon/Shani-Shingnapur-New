## What a stotra is

A *stotra* is a hymn of praise, usually in Sanskrit verse. Where a mantra is short and repeated, a stotra is longer and is read or sung through, praising the deity's qualities and asking for grace. There are several well-known stotras and hymns for Shani Dev.

On this page we explain each text's background and themes. We quote only very short, traditional lines; for the full text, use a printed edition from a reputable publisher or a version given by your family or temple tradition, as wording varies between editions.

## Dasharatha Shani Stotra

The most celebrated Shani hymn is attributed to **King Dasharatha** of Ayodhya, the father of Rama.

:::belief The story behind the hymn
According to tradition, astrologers warned Dasharatha that Shani's movement would bring a long famine to his kingdom. Dasharatha set out to confront Shani himself. Impressed by the king's courage and his concern for his people, Shani offered him a boon, and Dasharatha praised Shani with this hymn. Shani then promised not to trouble those who recite it with devotion.
:::

The stotra opens with the line:

<p class="devanagari" lang="sa">नमः कृष्णाय नीलाय शितिकण्ठनिभाय च ।</p>

*Namah krishnaya nilaya shitikantha-nibhaya cha* — "Salutations to the dark one, the blue one, who resembles the blue-throated (Shiva)."

The hymn goes on to praise Shani's dark form, his fearsome gaze and his power, before asking for his mercy. Its central message — that humility and devotion soften even a stern judge — has made it a favourite during periods such as Sade Sati.

## Navagraha Stotra (Shani verse)

The *Navagraha Stotra*, traditionally attributed to the sage **Vyasa**, contains one verse for each of the nine planetary deities. The Shani verse, beginning *Nilanjana samabhasam*, is given in full with its meaning on our [Shani mantra](/shani-dev/mantra/) page. Many devotees recite the whole stotra daily to honour all nine *grahas*.

## Shani Ashtottara Shatanamavali (108 names)

This text lists **108 names of Shani Dev**, each followed by *namah*. The names describe his qualities — for example Shanaishchara ("the slow-moving"), Surya-putra ("son of the Sun"), Chhaya-suta ("son of Chhaya") and Manda ("the slow"). Reciting the 108 names is a common part of Saturday pooja.

## Shani Chalisa

The *Shani Chalisa* is a devotional hymn in **Hindi**, following the familiar *chalisa* form of forty verses (*chaupais*) with opening and closing couplets. It retells Shani's qualities and stories in simple language and is especially popular in northern and central India. Its authorship is not reliably recorded, and printed versions differ.

## Shani Kavach

A *kavach* ("armour") is a protective hymn in which the devotee asks the deity to guard each part of the body. A *Shani Kavach* is recited by some devotees; like many such texts, its attribution to specific Puranic sources varies between editions.

## Reciting stotras

- **When:** Saturday mornings or evenings are traditional.
- **How:** Read slowly and clearly; understanding the meaning is valued more than speed.
- **With what:** Often in front of a sesame-oil lamp, a picture of Shani Dev, or at a Hanuman or Shani shrine.
- **In a group:** Many temples and families recite stotras together on Saturdays or on [Shani Jayanti](/shani-dev/shani-jayanti/).

As with mantras, devotees value stotras for the peace and focus they bring. We do not suggest that any recitation guarantees a particular outcome — see [the significance of Shani Dev](/shani-dev/significance/).
