## The custom before 2016

For generations, the custom at Shani Shingnapur did not permit women to go onto the raised platform where the stone of Shani Dev stands. Women could take darshan from a distance, but not approach the stone or offer oil directly. Supporters of the custom described it as a long-standing tradition of the shrine; critics saw it as discrimination.

## A national debate

In late 2015 an incident in which a woman went onto the platform, followed by ritual "purification" of the stone, drew wide media attention and intensified public debate about the custom.

In **January 2016**, activists of the **Bhumata Ranragini Brigade**, led by **Trupti Desai**, set out for Shingnapur to demand the right to go onto the platform. They were stopped by police before reaching the village. The debate — which also involved other places of worship in India with similar restrictions — continued in the media, in politics and in the courts.

## The Bombay High Court

A public interest litigation raised the question before the Bombay High Court. On **1 April 2016**, a division bench observed that there was no law preventing women from entering a place of worship where men were allowed, and that the state government had a duty to ensure women were not denied entry.

## The trust's decision

On **8 April 2016** — the festival of Gudi Padwa — the temple trust decided to allow **all devotees, women and men**, onto the platform. Women devotees went onto the platform to worship later that day.

## What it means for visitors today

- **All devotees may approach the platform**, subject to the crowd-control arrangements in place on the day.
- **Arrangements for offerings** — such as how oil is offered — are set by the trust and can change with crowd levels. Follow the instructions of temple staff.
- **Views still differ.** Some devotees continue to hold traditional views; others welcomed the change. Visitors are asked simply to worship respectfully and follow current rules.

## Why it matters

The Shingnapur decision became one of the best-known moments in a wider national conversation about religious custom and equal access to places of worship. For many women devotees, it meant being able to offer worship to Shani Dev directly for the first time.
